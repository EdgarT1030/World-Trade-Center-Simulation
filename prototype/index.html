What is computed and what is only drawn

Computed. Roof sway from the plane's momentum. Fire temperature at the wall on each of seven floors. Steel temperature of each floor truss and outer column, every 5 seconds. Which floors have sagged (truss above 600 °C). How far the outer columns span without support, and their buckling strength at their current temperature. Whether the floors below can catch the falling block, and how long the fall takes if they cannot.

Set by you, not predicted. How many floors lost their insulation, and how long the fire takes to reach the wall that fails. NIST says the time to collapse was governed by how long the fires took to reach and weaken that wall. [1] The default is set so the two real cases land near the real collapse times of 102 and 56 minutes. This model cannot predict those times on its own.

Estimates inside the model. Fire reaches each floor above and below 5 minutes after its neighbour. A spot burns at peak for 5 minutes per lb/sq ft of contents and burns out after about 22 minutes per lb/sq ft, which matches NIST's note that 4 lb/sq ft gives a fire of about an hour and a half. [7] Outer columns are heated on their one inside face and lose heat from the other three, so they run much cooler than the trusses. Column plates are assumed thicker lower in the tower. Extra load on the wall scales with the plane's energy.

    This is a hand-calculation model. It is not a finite-element simulation like the one NIST ran, and it cannot reproduce the collapse frame by frame. What it can do is test whether each link in the chain is physically possible, and show how far an input has to move before a link breaks.

    The building and the day

    What was standing there

    • Size. 110 storeys, 1,368 ft (WTC 1) and 1,362 ft (WTC 2), about 207 ft square, roughly 12 ft per storey. About 40,000 sq ft per floor. [1][8]
    • Outer wall. 236 steel box columns, 59 per face, each about 14 in square and 40 in apart, tied together at every floor by 52-in-deep steel plates. This wall resisted the wind and carried about half the weight. [1]
    • Core. 47 heavier columns in a 135 ft by 87 ft rectangle around the elevators and stairs, carrying the other half. [1]
    • Floors. 4 in of lightweight concrete on steel trusses spanning 60 ft or 35 ft from core to outer wall, with no columns in between. The floors also held the outer columns in line. [1]
    • Hat truss. Steel bracing from floor 107 to the roof tying core and outer wall together. [1]
    • Fire protection. Sprayed insulation on the steel: about ¾ in on the floor trusses in WTC 2's fire floors, about 2½ in on the upgraded WTC 1 fire floors. [2]
    • The day. Clear, with wind from the north at roughly 11 to 22 mph at tower height. About 8,900 people in WTC 1 and 8,500 in WTC 2. NIST puts the towers at one-third to one-half full. [1][6][8]
    • Timeline. WTC 1 hit 8:46 a.m., fell 10:28 a.m. (102 minutes). WTC 2 hit 9:02 a.m., fell 9:58 a.m. (56 minutes). [2]
    Check 1 of 7

    Did the plane's weight overload the building?

    Weights compared, million lb
    Show the math

    NIST gives the floor loading directly: the connections holding one floor to the columns could carry about 29 million lb, and the floor's everyday load was about 2.5 million lb. [2] The plane's whole weight is small next to that spare capacity.

    Check 2 of 7

    Did the impact knock the tower over, or shake it apart?

    Sideways movement at the roof, inches
    This modelNIST figure
    Show the math

    The tower is treated as one swinging mass with the 11.4-second period NIST measured from video. [4] The plane hands over its momentum in one push, and the tower swings back and forth. This is my model, and it can be checked: NIST measured WTC 2 swaying 12 in (±1) at floor 70 and about 22 in (±5) at the roof after impact. [4] Set the tower to WTC 2 and compare.

    The swaying died down within minutes. NIST notes that once it stopped, everything that followed happened at near-zero speed, so the collapse an hour or more later was not a dynamic effect of the hit. [2]

    Check 3 of 7

    Did the wind that morning matter?

    Show the math

    The same swinging-mass model gives the tower's sideways stiffness. As a cross-check, NIST calculated that the original design wind loads would move the top 51 to 65 in. [6] The readout shows how strong a wind this model needs to match that. NIST left wind out of its collapse analysis after checking airport wind records for the day. [5]

    Check 4 of 7

    Was there enough fuel to make a serious fire?

    Heat available inside the tower, gigajoules
    Show the math

    NIST reports that less than 15% of the jet fuel burned in the spray cloud inside the building and a comparable amount in the fireballs outside, so well over half was left to burn inside. [1] It also says the fires were started by the jet fuel and fed mostly by the building's contents, and that hydrocarbon and ordinary building fires both top out near 1,100 °C. [2] An independent check: NIST estimated from the smoke plume that each tower fire released heat at a rate on the order of one gigawatt. [8] Compare that with the average rate above.

    Can an office fire get steel hot enough to matter?

    Estimate Thin steel heats fast, thick steel slowly. Surface-to-volume ratios of about 300, 160 and 40 per metre, from the plate and bar sizes NIST describes.

    This is the standard Eurocode 3 hand method for steel in a fire, stepped every 5 seconds. Steel strength at temperature also comes from Eurocode 3: full strength to 400 °C, 47% at 600 °C, 11% at 800 °C, 4% at 1,000 °C. NIST's own summary is that bare steel at 1,000 °C keeps roughly 10% of its strength and that structural steel melts near 1,500 °C. [2] Nothing here melts. It does not need to.

    NIST's conclusion rests on the insulation: it found the towers would likely have survived the impacts and fires if the insulation had not been knocked off. [1] Set the insulation thickness to see how much difference it makes in this model.

    Check 6 of 7

    Can hot, sagging floors make the outer columns buckle?

    What one outer column can carry, kips (1 kip = 1,000 lb)
    Your settingOther unbraced heights (number of floors)
    Show the math

    Each outer column was a slender 14-in box. It was strong because a floor held it in line every 12 ft. When floors sag or let go, the column has to span two, three or more storeys unbraced, and its buckling strength drops with the square of that length. Heat lowers the steel's stiffness on top of that. The formula is the AISC 360 column curve with Eurocode 3 hot-steel properties.

    This is where NIST places the start of both collapses: sagging floors pulled the outer wall inward until it buckled. [2] The inward bowing was measured from photographs before each collapse: up to 55 in on the south face of WTC 1 at 10:23 a.m., five minutes before it fell, and 10 in growing to 20 in on the east face of WTC 2. [5] Compare the bow estimate above. A separate engineering study for the leaseholder agreed on impact and fire as the cause but argued that column failures alone, without the floors, explain the collapses. [9]

    Check 7 of 7

    Once the top starts to fall, can the floors below stop it?

    Floors
    Show the math

    The first part is NIST's own back-of-envelope check, with its numbers on the sliders. [2] The fall times are my calculation: the upper block drops one storey at a time, and each floor it lands on joins the falling mass (momentum is conserved, speed drops a little each time). The floors' own strength is ignored, so the real time would be somewhat longer. NIST reports the first outer wall panels reached the ground about 11 seconds (WTC 1) and 9 seconds (WTC 2) after collapse began, and that parts of both cores stood for 15 to 25 seconds. [2]

    NIST did not build a detailed computer model of the collapse after it started. It stopped at the point of initiation and argued the rest from energy, as above. [2] Critics count that as a gap.

    Read before trusting any of this

    What this sheet cannot tell you

    • It is seven hand calculations, not a simulation. Real fires move, real floors pull unevenly, and loads shift between columns in ways one formula cannot follow.
    • Several inputs are my estimates. The tower's weight per storey, the column plate thickness at the impact floors, the insulation's conductivity and how long one spot stayed at peak temperature are not taken from a source. They are tagged, and the results move when you move them.
    • Flight 175's weight is assumed equal to Flight 11's. Its fuel load is reported between about 8,200 and 10,000 gallons depending on the source. [8]
    • The severed-column counts come from NIST's impact simulations, not from inspection. The building interiors could not be examined afterward. NIST says most physical evidence was destroyed or removed before its investigation began. [2]
    • The recovered steel does not confirm the temperatures. NIST found only three of more than 170 examined outer-panel locations had exceeded 250 °C, and no sample showed long exposure above 600 °C. NIST says the samples were 3% of the outer columns on the fire floors and are not representative. [13]
    • It covers the Twin Towers only. Building 7, which was not hit by a plane, needs its own analysis.
    • Physics cannot settle questions of who knew what. Those belong to the evidence library, built on documents and testimony.
    Warnings record

    What the government knew, and when

    Two separate questions get mixed together. Did the government know an attack was coming? And did anyone know planes were heading for the towers that morning in time to stop them? The public record answers them differently.

    • Before the day: the record shows years of warnings that al Qaeda wanted to attack inside the United States, several that mentioned hijackings or aircraft as weapons, and specific leads on future hijackers that were not acted on.
    • On the day: air defense controllers had nine minutes' notice of the first plane and none of the other three, according to the 9/11 Commission's reconstruction from tapes and logs. [10]
    • What is missing: no document made public so far shows anyone in the government knowing the date, the targets or the method in advance. That is the gap between "they had warnings" and "they knew."
    Documented In an official record or a declassified document. Disputed Real events, contested meaning. Not supported Investigated and not borne out, as far as the public record shows.

    How to read this page: the strongest documented case is that the government had repeated warnings and specific leads, and failed to connect them. That case rests on the government's own records. The claim that officials knew the date and targets, and let it happen, needs a document or witness the public record does not yet contain. More records may yet be released, and this section should be updated as they appear.
